Over the past decade, research has moved from a culture in which findings were taken on trust towards one in which they have to be shown. What began as a response to the ‘replication crisis’ has grown from a niche advocacy movement into a widely adopted standard for credible research. Brian Nosek, of the Center for Open Science, draws the distinction in a Nature Communications Q&A, ‘Reproducibility and transparency: What’s going on and how can we help’, where he describes science as ‘a show-me enterprise, not a trust-me enterprise’. Other recent pieces reflect the same shift, among them the Nature Materials editorial ‘Trust but verify’ and an article in The Conversation, ‘Reproducibility may be the key idea students need to balance trust in evidence with healthy skepticism’.

The Mechanics of Transparency

Two practices are central: preregistration and the open sharing of data and materials. Preregistration means publicly archiving the theoretical background, methods and hypotheses of a study before data collection begins. It guards against questionable research practices such as ‘p-hacking’ (adjusting analyses until a result is significant) and ‘HARKing’ (hypothesising after the results are known), and it separates planned confirmatory research from exploratory work.

Both practices benefit each party to a project. For funders, openly shared data and materials go on yielding value long after the final report is filed. Funders can also show a commitment to corporate social responsibility by having the data they fund shared under a suitable licence. The Creative Commons Attribution-NonCommercial-NoDerivatives 4.0 International Public License (CC BY-NC-ND 4.0), for example, protects intellectual credit by requiring attribution, and it rules out commercial use and the sharing of adapted versions. Other licences strike a different balance. CC BY 4.0 allows any use, commercial use included, provided the authors are credited. A repository on the Open Science Framework (OSF) can name its funders prominently and link to them, documenting their role, as in this example repository.

For the project itself, these practices make its rigour visible. They set the work apart from studies whose methods remain a ‘black box’, and they show peer reviewers and collaborators that its findings can be checked.

For researchers, especially early in their careers, sharing data and code on platforms such as the OSF builds a public, citable portfolio of technical work. The portfolio shows a capacity for careful, transparent research and can lead to recognition and collaboration that a CV alone would not bring.

Finally, for the wider scientific community, a dataset shared with the analyses behind it allows others to verify the findings independently and to reuse the data for new questions, which extends the reach of the original funding.

A Commitment to Integrity

Open science is a commitment to the idea that the best science is done in the light. Transparency makes published findings permanent and verifiable, and at a time of widespread misinformation, findings that anyone can check carry particular weight. The aim is research that remains sound, reusable and trustworthy long after publication.
