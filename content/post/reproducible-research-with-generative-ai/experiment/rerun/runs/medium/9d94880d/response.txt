I wrote and ran `analysis.R`. It estimates the priming effect at **18.5 ms, 95% CI [3.6, 33.4]**. Responses after unrelated primes were slower, and the effect is reliable (p = .015). The raw means agree: 698.7 ms after related primes and 717.2 ms after unrelated primes. The last line it prints is:

`RESULT estimate=18.52 lower=3.63 upper=33.41`

**What the script does:**
1. It reads `data.csv` and checks that the columns are present, there are no missing values, and `prime` has only the two levels. It then prints the counts (1,440 trials, 30 participants, 24 items) and the mean response time for each prime condition.
2. It fits a mixed-effects model (lme4/lmerTest) to raw response times, so the estimate comes out in milliseconds. Participants and items are random effects. Prime is coded −0.5 for related and +0.5 for unrelated, so its coefficient is the unrelated minus related difference. Zipf frequency, centred, is a covariate. It only explains differences between items, so it doesn't bias the priming estimate.
3. It tries the full model first, with the priming effect allowed to vary by participant and by item. If a model gives a warning or a singular fit, it drops to a simpler one. Every richer model was singular here: the data show essentially no variation in the priming effect across participants or items. So the model kept has only random intercepts for participants and items.
4. It takes the 95% CI from a t distribution with Satterthwaite degrees of freedom (SE = 7.6, df ≈ 1386), prints the model summary and a readable result line, and ends with the `RESULT` line.

The model's residuals are right-skewed, as usual for response times. I kept raw milliseconds because the question asks for the effect in ms, so the CI rests on a normality assumption that the residuals only roughly meet.
