**Priming effect: about 18.5 ms, 95% CI about −1.5 to 38.6 ms.** Responses after unrelated primes were on average 18.5 ms slower than after related primes. The interval runs from about 1.5 ms of speed-up to about 38.6 ms of slow-down. It includes zero, so the effect is positive but not reliably different from zero once differences between words are taken into account.

I read the file and did all the arithmetic by hand; nothing was run. The data are complete: 30 participants × 24 targets × 2 prime types = 1,440 rows, all participant–target pairs balanced.

**How I got there**
1. For each participant–target pair I took the difference: unrelated RT minus related RT (720 differences).
2. The differences add up to 13,334 ms, both when totalled by participant and when totalled by target, which cross-checks the sums. The mean is 13,334 / 720 = **18.52 ms**. I also re-summed one participant's raw RTs directly and it matched. Because the design is fully balanced, a mixed model gives this same estimate, and adding target frequency as a covariate doesn't change it.
3. To get the uncertainty I split the variation in those 720 differences into three parts:
   - **Participants:** 28,810 (29 degrees of freedom).
   - **Targets:** 67,640 (23 degrees of freedom).
   - **Leftover noise:** 41,047 (667 degrees of freedom).
4. Participants' priming effects vary no more than noise alone would produce, so their variance comes out as zero. Targets do differ in how much priming they show: the standard deviation of the priming effect across targets is about 30 ms. That is roughly the model `rt ~ prime (+ frequency) + (1 + prime | participant) + (1 + prime | item)`, which counts both participants and targets as random samples.
5. Under that model the standard error is √(67,640 / 720) = **9.69 ms**. With 23 degrees of freedom, t = 2.069, so the interval is 18.5 ± 20.1 → **[−1.5, 38.6]**. Using the normal approximation instead gives [−0.5, 37.5].

**Why narrower answers would be too optimistic**
- **Averaging over targets within each participant** (a paired t-test across participants) gives 18.5 [5.6, 31.5]. This ignores that the targets are a sample too.
- **Letting only average speed vary** (random intercepts for participants and targets, no per-target priming effect) gives about 18.5 [3.6, 33.4].

Both of those intervals exclude zero, but only because they leave out the target-to-target differences in priming. Those differences are real, and a test of that variation comes out at p ≈ .03.

**Things to keep in mind**
- A mixed-model program's exact bounds could differ from mine by about 1 ms. That would come from how it estimates the correlations between random effects and the noise term.
- The response times are right-skewed, with several above 1,400 ms. Analysing them on a log scale would give a somewhat different result.
